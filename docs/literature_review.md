# Literature review (status notes, 2026-10-13)

**Status: partial.** Seven of the nine topic areas named in the overhaul brief have not yet been
searched. This document covers what was verified in the first pass (the eight named sources plus
two found while verifying them) and names explicitly what is still open. Every citation below was
checked against at least one authoritative source via web search on 2026-10-13 (see
`references/references.bib` for the verification note on each). Nothing below is invented.

## 1. What is already established (do not reinvent)

**Recognition as a relational, not a possessed, property.** Duque (duque2018recognizing) models
status as something *granted* through relations (diplomatic representation) rather than read off
a state's own attributes -- her ERGM approach to embassy-formation networks is the methodological
ancestor of any design (including this project's) that wants to measure recognition through what
an incumbent *does* (covers, sends an ambassador to, frames as a peer) rather than what a
challenger *has*. This motivates the material/perceived split in Section III of the overhaul
brief: it is already a recognized best practice, not a novel move.

**Resource-recognition gaps are the norm, not an anomaly to be modeled away.**
Røren and Beaumont (roren2019grading) build a status-performance index (recognition achieved given
material resources) for the five BRICS states and argue explicitly that gaps between resources and
recognition should be *explained through case studies*, not treated as measurement error to be
regressed out. This directly supports the overhaul brief's rejection of a single aggregate
"recognition lag" number (Section III.5) in favor of domain-specific, case-specific turning points.

**Status deficits, not status levels, are the behaviorally consequential quantity.**
Renshon (renshon2016status, renshon2017fighting) shows, across a large interstate dataset, that
the *gap* between a state's material capability rank and its recognized diplomatic rank predicts
conflict initiation -- and that reference groups ("status communities") are local, not global. This
is directly relevant to case selection: it is a documented empirical regularity that justifies
treating Japan/Korea, Korea/China, Korea/Vietnam as separate local status communities rather than
comparable points on one global hierarchy.

**A specific, falsifiable mechanism for "hierarchy reproduction" already exists in print.**
Claudia Junghyun Kim's "stigma shifting" (kim2024status) is, as far as this search found, the
closest existing formalization of the overhaul brief's M5 mechanism: status-anxious states manage
stigma-induced inferiority by seeking identification with higher-status states and actively
differentiating from lower-status ones, and Kim applies this specifically to East Asian cases
(colonial redress, nuclear-disaster framing, order-making). This project's "does Korea, having
once sought recognition from Japan, now withhold it from China" question is a direct empirical
extension of Kim's mechanism into a new domain (economic/industrial status rather than Kim's three
issue areas) and should engage with this article explicitly rather than treat hierarchy
reproduction as a novel conjecture.

**Discursive self-positioning in the challenger state is itself status-theoretic, not just
rhetoric.** Jongtae Kim (kim2014developmentalist) shows, via Korean newspaper-editorial analysis,
that Korean public discourse has long organized the world into *seonjinguk* ("advanced countries")
and an implicit residual category, with Korea positioned as perpetually "approaching but not yet
arrived." This is evidence for a real, pre-existing Korean self-concept of developmental
hierarchy that predates and plausibly conditions how Korea now talks about China's rise -- directly
relevant to the kor_chn case and to interpreting any "hierarchy reproduction" finding (Korea's
framing of China may echo how Korea believed Japan/the West once framed Korea, independent of any
causal process).

**Public status concerns are not confined to elites.** Powers and Renshon (powers2023international)
show, via seven preregistered survey experiments, that ordinary citizens respond to expected
status loss from foreign-policy outcomes by lowering approval of their own leaders, and that
open-ended survey responses show the public reasoning about status in the same
multidimensional/positional terms IR scholars use. This supports treating public-opinion survey
items (Section III.7 of the brief) as a genuine, theoretically motivated recognition measure, not
merely a convenient proxy.

**The EAI/Genron Korea-Japan Joint Perception Survey is a precedented, actively used data source
for exactly this kind of question.** Seongjo Kim (kim2025geopolitics) used the 2022 wave directly
to show that favorability/interdependence predict status perceptions among Japanese respondents
only, while geopolitical factors (China threat perception, US importance) predict status
perceptions in both directions. This is strong independent confirmation that (a) the survey asks
status-perception-relevant items, (b) it is accessible enough to support peer-reviewed publication,
and (c) bilateral and regional/geopolitical channels need to be modeled separately rather than
pooled -- a direct design implication for this project's M2 (status threat) vs. M1 (material
convergence) distinction.

## 2. What remains genuinely open (not resolved by the above)

- **Timing mechanism.** None of the verified sources estimate *when* a recognition transition
  happens relative to a material-parity date for a specific dyad -- the turning-point estimation
  this project is built around (Section IV of the brief) is not something this literature already
  answers. This is consistent with the brief's framing of it as the contribution.
- **East Asia-specific developmental-hierarchy literature, as a status-theoretic body of work
  distinct from developmental-state institutional economics (Johnson 1982, Cumings), was not
  found** in this pass. The search surfaced developmental-state scholarship generally but nothing
  that treats "developmental hierarchy" as a status/recognition construct the way this project
  needs. Needs a follow-up search combining status-theory terms with Kang's "China Rising" /
  East Asian hierarchy scholarship and David Kang's broader work, and with Victoria Tin-bor Hui or
  Suzuki on East Asian international society -- not yet done.
- **Status inconsistency (the classical mid-20th-century sociology literature, Galtung and
  successors) was not searched** in this pass, despite being named in the brief (Section II.3.2).
- **Social distance, cultural prestige, generational change, and recognition-of-technological-
  leadership-as-a-status-construct (Section II.3.6, .7, .8) were not searched** in this pass.
